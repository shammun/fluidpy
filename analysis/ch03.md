## 1. Chapter objectives and outline (our words)

**What the chapter is for (paraphrasing the chapter objectives):**
1. Recap the coordinate systems (plane Cartesian/polar, 3-D Cartesian, cylindrical, spherical) and the names of their velocity components.
2. Connect the motion of a fluid to the kinematics of a single particle — the Lagrangian (follow the particle) and Eulerian (watch a fixed point) descriptions, and the bridge between them.
3. Define the three kinds of flow line (streamline, path line, streak line) and show why they differ when the flow is unsteady.
4. Write the acceleration of a fluid particle in Eulerian variables (the material derivative) and show it does not change under a Galilean change of frame.
5. Give the strain-rate and rotation tensors a physical meaning (stretching, shearing, volume change, spin).
6. Give a rule for differentiating a volume integral whose volume moves and deforms (Leibniz → Reynolds transport theorem) — the tool Chapter 4 needs to turn conservation laws into equations.

**Outline:** 3.1 steady/unsteady, 1-/2-/3-D flows (Fig. 3.1, 3.2), coordinate systems (Fig. 3.3) · 3.2 Lagrangian description (3.1), compatibility with the Eulerian description (3.2), chain rule (3.3) → material derivative (3.4)–(3.6) · 3.3 streamlines (3.7), stream tubes, path lines (3.8), streak lines, Example 3.1 (the three lines in an oscillating flow, Fig. 3.7), Galilean transformation and invariance of the acceleration (3.9, Fig. 3.8), local vs advective terms (traffic-light and roller-coaster analogies) · 3.4 relative motion near a point (3.10), S + ½R split (3.11)–(3.13), linear strain rate (Fig. 3.10), shear strain rate (Fig. 3.11), volumetric strain rate (3.14), vorticity (3.15)–(3.16), rotation rate = ½ω, irrotational flow (3.17), circulation (3.18), relative velocity = deformation + rigid rotation (3.19), principal axes (3.20)–(3.21), sphere → ellipsoid (Fig. 3.13) · 3.5 parallel shear flow (Fig. 3.14), solid-body rotation (3.22)–(3.24), irrotational vortex (3.25)–(3.27), Rankine and Gaussian vortices (3.28)–(3.29) · 3.6 Leibniz's theorem (3.30), Reynolds transport theorem (3.31)–(3.35), Example 3.2 (growing cone) · 30 exercises · literature.

## 9. Risks, conventions, ambiguities, suspected typos

**Suspected typos / printing artefacts (code what is correct; record "book prints X; implemented Y"):**
1. **(3.6)** prints $\partial F/\partial t+\lvert\mathbf u\rvert\,\partial/\partial s$ — the F is missing from the second term (confirmed at 300 dpi). Correct: $\lvert\mathbf u\rvert\,\partial F/\partial s$ (both terms must be rates of change of F).
2. **Example 3.2** integrand prints "z [?] tan θ dφ" — a placeholder artefact; the factor is simply z (the circumference radius R = z tan θ). The final answer is unaffected (sympy re-derivation: 2πh²ṙ tan θ/3 = 2πh r_o ṙ/3).
3. **Example 3.2** says b = 0 on the cone's base; the base's points actually move radially (the disc grows). Only b·n = 0 there is true, which is all RTT needs. Say so in the notebook.
4. **§3.4 cross-reference** "principal axes of the tensor (see Section 2.12 and Example 2.4)": principal axes are §2.11 (§2.12 is Gauss' theorem). §3.5 cites §2.11 correctly.
5. **§3.4** "equations (3.11) through (3.14) allow (3.10) to be rewritten as (3.19)" — the step uses (3.15) (R ↔ ω), not (3.14).
6. **§3.5** "a linear compression rate of −γ/2" is a double negative: the eigenvalue is −γ/2, i.e. a compression rate of γ/2. Use "eigenvalue −γ/2 (compression)".
7. **Fig. 3.16** labels the velocity u_θ = C/r while the text's (3.25) uses B. Use B throughout.
8. **Exercises 3.1/3.2** point at Fig. 3.3b/3.3c for cylindrical/spherical coordinates; they are Fig. 3.3c/3.3d. (Pointer only; exercise text is never published.)
9. Extraction traps specific to this chapter: ω prints as `u` (so "u ¼ V  u" is ω = ∇ × u), ξ as `x` (Ex. 3.1's amplitude ξ_o prints as "xo", colliding with the integration constant x_o!), t′ prints as `t0` (colliding with the release time t_o in the streak-line part), φ as `4`, σ as `s`, Γ as `G`, γ as `g`. All LaTeX above is from the images.

**Conventions and sign traps:**
- **Factor 2 (carried from ch02):** the book's R = G − Gᵀ (no ½), so G = S + ½R and ω = vector of R; the element spin is ½ω. ch02's `rotation_tensor` already returns G − Gᵀ (review M1). Keep the "spin ½ω₃" readout and the parity rows "vorticity = vector(R)".
- **Two different γ/Γ:** ch02 Ex. 2.4 used Γ ≡ S₁₂ (so λ = ±Γ); ch03 §3.5 uses γ = du₁/dx₂ = 2S₁₂ (so λ = ±γ/2). And Γ in §3.4–3.5 is **circulation** [m²/s]. Notation register needs three rows (⚠️).
- **Sign of ω₃ in shear:** u = (γx₂, 0) with γ > 0 has ω₃ = −γ (clockwise spin). Figures must show the paddle turning clockwise.
- **α and β in Figs. 3.11:** α is the clockwise tilt of the vertical side, β the counter-clockwise tilt of the horizontal side; the strain rate uses α + β (closing), the rotation rate −α + β. Code with signed angles measured counter-clockwise and derive both from them.
- **Spherical angles:** book uses (r, θ, φ) with θ from +z (polar) and φ azimuth (physics convention; Fig. 3.3d); cylindrical uses (R, φ, z) with capital R, but plane polar and §3.5 use (r, θ). Our code names must say which (`theta_polar`, `phi_azimuth`).
- **"t′" in Ex. 3.1** is the drawing instant; t_o is a release time; x_o, y_o are integration constants; ξ_o is the amplitude. Keep four distinct names in code (`t_prime`, `t_release`, `c_x/c_y`, `xi0`).
- **Galilean vs rotating frames:** (3.9) holds only for constant U. In a rotating frame the acceleration is *not* invariant (Coriolis, centrifugal: Ch. 4 §4.7, Ch. 13) and ω changes by 2Ω (#44) — warn explicitly, this is the climate-relevant case (relative vs absolute vorticity).
- **Frame dependence claims:** S is unchanged by any rigid-motion change of frame (even time-dependent translation and rotation), ω is not. The book's sentence "S_ij is independent of the frame … even if U depends on time" refers to *components after rotating back*; teach it as "adding a rigid motion changes G by an antisymmetric part only".
- **Signed swept volume in RTT:** where b·n < 0 the sliver volume counts negatively; one formula covers inflow and outflow — say it at D30, it is the most common student confusion.

**Equations stated without derivation or with the proof deferred to an exercise** (we write them out, §2b): (3.7) Ex. 3.3; (3.9) Ex. 3.12; S = 0 for rigid motion Ex. 3.17; (3.14) Ex. 3.18; ω′ = ω − 2Ω Ex. 3.19; (3.23) Ex. 3.20 / Appendix B; Γ for any circuit Ex. 3.23; 1.12091σ Ex. 3.26; RTT → (3.14) Ex. 3.28; RTT → (3.5) Ex. 3.30; sphere → ellipsoid (no proof at all); Leibniz (3.30) (cited to Riley et al.); Rankine/Gaussian consistency (stated).

**Silently switching assumptions / hidden hypotheses:**
- (3.17) "u can be written as ∇φ" needs a **simply connected** region; the line vortex (3.25) — in the same chapter — is irrotational off-axis yet has ∮u·ds = 2πB, so φ = Bθ is multivalued. Our `velocity_potential_2d` must report the path-dependence rather than assert.
- RTT → (3.5) (Ex. 3.30): with b = u the RTT gives DF/Dt + F∇·u per unit volume; reducing to (3.5) requires subtracting F × (3.14). The text's "extension of (3.5) to finite volumes" hides this — make the extra term visible (it becomes the continuity-equation trick of Ch. 4).
- Ex. 3.1's field is spatially uniform (u independent of x), so every particle traces the *same* circle shape — a caricature of the orbital motion under long waves (real orbits decay with depth and have phase varying with x; Ch. 7). Say so; do not call it a wave solution.
- Sphere → ellipsoid is a first-order-in-dt statement. For finite time in a steady linear shear flow the ellipse's axes rotate away from the 45° principal axes (they follow the singular vectors of e^{Gt}); an animation that runs long will "disagree" with the 45° claim unless this is explained.
- The Gaussian vortex (3.29) is steady here but is the Lamb–Oseen solution with σ(t) = √(4νt) in Ch. 5 — pointer only.

**Numerical risks:**
- Streamline integration by arc length stalls at stagnation points (\lvert u\rvert → 0): terminal event + report.
- Streak lines need many path lines: vectorise the ensemble (one ODE of size 3N) or the explainer/notebook budget blows; for explainers use RK4 in JS with the same field functions and parity rows against `pathline`.
- `gaussian_vortex` near r = 0: (1 − e^{−x})/r cancels; use `-np.expm1(-x)/r` and return 0 at r = 0.
- Line vortex and `cylinder_flow` are singular (r = 0, r < a): NaN-mask and `singular_at` (ch02 `VectorField`) so Stokes checks refuse to include the core.
- RTT checks by finite differences of a quadrature: use the same quadrature rule at t ± dt (nodes move with the shape) to avoid O(h) noise swamping O(dt²).

**Teaching gaps the notebook should fill (not book errors):** a concrete Lagrangian map with its Eulerian twin (D01); why the average of two perpendicular lines defines spin (D13/D20); the 1-D Leibniz rule as the special case of RTT (two end points); the climate hook — thermal advection −u·∇T as the advective part of DT/Dt, relative vs planetary vorticity (#44).
