## 1. Chapter objectives and outline (our words; what is new vs restated from earlier chapters)

**What the chapter is for (paraphrasing the seven objectives):**
1. Say what turbulent flow is and what distinguishes it from merely random motion.
2. Define the statistics used to describe it: averages, moments, correlations, spectra, and the scales read from them.
3. Derive the equations for the mean flow by Reynolds averaging, and see why they are not closed.
4. State the assumptions and dimensional arguments behind the classical scaling laws (cascade, Kolmogorov scales, −5/3
   spectrum, self-similar free shear flows, law of the wall and logarithmic law).
5. Give usable mean-flow summaries for free shear flows (jets, wakes, plumes, shear layers) and wall flows (channel, pipe,
   boundary layer).
6. Introduce turbulence modelling: eddy viscosity, mixing length, one- and two-equation (k–ε) closures.
7. Describe what stratification does to turbulence in the atmosphere and ocean (Richardson numbers, Monin–Obukhov length,
   temperature spectra) — plus, in the last section, Taylor's theory of how turbulence disperses particles.

**Outline and what is new.**
- §12.1 Introduction — five marks of turbulence (fluctuations, nonlinearity, vorticity, dissipation, diffusivity), a working
  definition, the largest eddy ≈ the layer thickness. *New*: the definition. *Restated*: Re as nonlinearity parameter (ch04,
  ch08), vortex stretching (ch05 (5.13)), transition (ch11).
- §12.2 Historical notes — Reynolds, Taylor, Prandtl, von Kármán, Richardson, Kolmogorov, Obukhov; previews every later result.
- §12.3 Nomenclature and statistics — ensemble, expected value, time and volume averages (12.1)–(12.3), the commutation rules
  (12.4)–(12.9), mean (12.10), central moments (12.11), Example 12.1. *All new.*
- §12.4 Correlations and spectra — correlation tensor and coefficients (12.12)–(12.15), Schwartz inequality (12.16), stationary
  forms (12.17), integral scale (12.18), Taylor microscale (12.19), spectrum as the Fourier transform of the autocorrelation
  (12.20)–(12.22), spatial correlation (12.23), Taylor's frozen-turbulence hypothesis. *New* (Fourier modes were used in ch07,
  ch10, ch11, but never a Fourier *transform pair*).
- §12.5 Averaged equations — Reynolds decomposition (12.24)–(12.26), mean continuity (12.27)–(12.28), RANS momentum (12.30)
  with the Reynolds stress, mean heat (12.31)–(12.32) and scalar (12.33)–(12.34) equations, the Reynolds-stress transport
  equation (12.35) and the closure problem. *New*; the starting set (4.10), (4.86), (4.89) is ch04.
- §12.6 Homogeneous isotropic turbulence — symmetry statements (12.36)–(12.37), longitudinal and transverse correlations
  (12.38)–(12.41), dissipation (12.42)–(12.43), Taylor-scale Reynolds number (12.44), one-dimensional spectrum (12.45).
  *New*; uses ch02 isotropic tensors and ch04 (4.58).
- §12.7 Energy cascade and spectrum — mean and turbulent kinetic-energy budgets (12.46)–(12.47), outer scaling of the
  dissipation (12.48)–(12.49), Kolmogorov scales (12.50)–(12.51), Taylor-scale scaling (12.52), universal spectrum
  (12.53)–(12.55). *New*; the production term is the turbulent version of ch11 (11.88).
- §12.8 Free shear flows — entrainment, self-preservation, the plane jet worked out (12.56)–(12.73), general similarity (12.74),
  a table of far-field laws for seven flows, Example 12.2, the jet's Reynolds stresses and energy budget (12.75). *New*; the
  method (invariant + similarity form + exponent matching) is ch08 §8.4 / ch09 §9.10.
- §12.9 Wall-bounded flows — total stress (12.76)–(12.78), law of the wall (12.79)–(12.82), defect law (12.83)–(12.84),
  logarithmic overlap (12.85)–(12.89), ZPG boundary-layer correlations, Example 12.3, composite profiles, pressure gradient and
  wall stress (12.90)–(12.91), the κ–B correlation (12.92), rough walls (12.93). *New.*
- §12.10 Modelling — eddy viscosity and gradient diffusion (12.94)–(12.98), mixing length (12.99)–(12.101), an eddy-diffusivity
  estimate (12.102), one-equation (12.103) and k–ε (12.104)–(12.105) models. *New.*
- §12.11 Stratified turbulence — reduced energy budget (12.106), flux and gradient Richardson numbers (12.107)–(12.109),
  Monin–Obukhov length (12.110)–(12.111), log-linear wind profile, temperature variance (12.112) and its spectra
  (12.113)–(12.114). *New*; N², Ri and the lapse-rate conventions are ch01, ch07, ch11.
- §12.12 Taylor's dispersion theory — (12.115)–(12.123), random walk (12.124)–(12.125), smoke plume, eddy diffusivity
  (12.126)–(12.129). *New*; Lagrangian description is ch03.
- §12.13 Concluding remarks (pointer to symposia). Exercises 12.1–12.38 (several *contain* the derivations the text skips:
  12.8, 12.9, 12.12, 12.15–12.19, 12.25–12.28, 12.32, 12.34).

**Mental model in one sentence:** "we cannot follow the motion, so we average it; averaging the nonlinear term leaves a
correlation we have no equation for (closure); dimensional analysis and similarity recover what the equations cannot give —
energy enters at the large scale at ΔU³/L, cascades without loss, and dies at η = (ν³/ε)^{1/4}; near a wall two length scales
overlap in a logarithm; in practice the missing correlation is modelled as an eddy viscosity, which stratification weakens
(Ri) and which Taylor's theory shows is not even a constant."

Climate hooks for the curator: eddy viscosity/diffusivity in every ocean and atmosphere model (ch13 Ekman layers need ν_T);
the logarithmic wind profile, roughness length z₀ and the Monin–Obukhov stability correction of surface-flux bulk formulas;
flux vs gradient Richardson number in mixing schemes; Kolmogorov scale of millimetres vs model grid of kilometres (why
turbulence must be parameterised; link to ch10 numerical diffusivity); Richardson's 4/3 law and dispersion of tracers and
pollutants; the Obukhov–Corrsin and Batchelor ranges of oceanic temperature microstructure; daytime convective vs nocturnal
stable boundary layer and the two lapse-rate sign conventions.

## 9. Risks, conventions, ambiguities, suspected typos

**C1. Lapse rate and "temperature" in §12.5, §12.7, §12.11 (project rule: compute with Kundu's Γ ≡ dT/dz, always show the
meteorological Γ = −dT/dz alongside).**
- The book says only at the start of §12.11 that the adiabatic part has been subtracted, so T̄ and T′ in the buoyancy terms of
  (12.30), (12.35), (12.47), (12.106), in the gradient Richardson number (12.108), in the variance budget (12.112) and in
  Figs. 12.10, 12.21, 12.22 are **potential** temperature. With the in-situ temperature the same quantities are
  N² = gα(dT/dz − Γ_a) with Γ_a = −g/C_p ≈ −9.76 K/km in Kundu's sign, i.e. N² = gα(Γ_d − Γ) with Γ_d ≈ +9.76 K/km and
  Γ = −dT/dz in the meteorological sign.
- Sentences of the type "unstable when the mean temperature decreases upward" (p. 597), "stable when it increases upward"
  (p. 565) and "the flux is positive if the gradient is negative" (p. 600) are true for θ. For the thermometer temperature the
  threshold is the adiabatic lapse rate: unstable ⇔ dT/dz < Γ_a (Kundu) ⇔ Γ > Γ_d (meteorology). An atmosphere cooling at
  6.5 K/km with height is **stable**; an isothermal layer is strongly stable; (12.108) evaluated with the in-situ gradient would
  call both of them wrong. `gradient_richardson_thermal(dTdz, …, Gamma_a=)` takes the Kundu-sign in-situ gradient, subtracts
  Γ_a, and returns the verdict text in both conventions through `core.stratification.lapse_rate_stability`; a test asserts the
  two routes (in-situ + Γ_a, and dθ/dz) agree.
- Heat-flux sign: $\overline{wT'}$ > 0 is upward (unstable, daytime), giving Rf < 0 and L_M < 0; meteorology writes
  L = −u_*³θ̄_v/(κg$\overline{w'\theta_v'}$), which is (12.110) with α = 1/θ̄ (and virtual temperature for moist air) — same sign.
  H [W/m²] = ρC_p$\overline{wT'}$.
- Notebook and explainer: the stability badge of ch01 (E4) reused; every profile plotted as T(z) **and** θ(z).

**C2. Two "one quarter" criteria that are not the same statement.** Ri > ¼ everywhere ⇒ *linear* stability of an inviscid
stratified shear flow (ch11 (11.67), sufficient condition, a theorem). Rf_cr ≈ ¼ is an *observed* value above which turbulence
cannot maintain itself; by (12.109) it corresponds to Ri = Pr_T/4, which can exceed 1 in stable conditions. Teach both with the
words "theorem" and "observation".

**C3. Overloaded symbols (a conventions table up front, enforced in every cell — the ch10 lesson).**

(the book's ē) — code `e` for turbulent kinetic energy (ch01's e = internal energy: say so), `k1`, `K` for the 3-D wavenumber
(also the kurtosis K of Exercise 12.1). ε̄ dissipation (ch04 ε), ε̄_T its thermal analogue. Ē mean-flow kinetic energy (ch11 E =
disturbance energy). λ: Taylor microscales λ_t, λ_f, λ_g, λ_T (earlier: wavelength, eigenvalue, Thwaites parameter). Λ: integral
scales (ch11 Λ = dissipation in (11.88)). η: Kolmogorov scale (earlier: similarity variable, surface elevation), η_T Batchelor
scale. τ: time lag (12.17), decay time (Example 12.1), stress τ̄, τ₀. f, g: correlation functions (12.38), f also the law-of-the-wall
function (12.80) and the Darcy friction factor, g gravity. F, G: tensor functions (12.40), jet profiles (12.56)–(12.57), F the
defect function (12.84). R_ij: correlation tensor (ch02–ch03: rotation tensor; the book warns once). r: separation, radius,
correlation coefficient r₁₁, r_α. δ: jet width, boundary-layer thickness, Kronecker delta; δ*, δ₉₉. θ: momentum thickness
(§12.9), potential temperature (ours, §12.11), θ_p, θ_r wake thicknesses. α: thermal expansion coefficient and the no-sum Greek
index of §12.12. Ψ: stress amplitude (12.57) (ch09: wall-jet invariant). Π: Coles' wake strength (ch01: Π groups). Φ: universal
spectrum function. S: spectra S_e, S₁₁, S_T, S(K) vs strain rates S̄_ij, S′_ij vs skewness. ω: angular frequency here (ch05:
vorticity). N: number of realizations and buoyancy frequency. L: outer scale, random-walk step, L_M. U₀: probe speed (§12.4) and
nozzle exit speed (§12.8). T: temperature, the transport term of Fig. 12.15, record length in Exercise 12.8. σ: Gaussian width

**C4. Normalisations that change.** (a) Spectra: the book's are two-sided in angular frequency or wavenumber with the 1/2π in the
forward transform, integrating to the variance over (−∞, ∞); Fig. 12.12 plots 2S₁₁ (one-sided); the quoted one-dimensional
constant is for the two-sided form, the three-dimensional one for ē = ∫₀^∞S(K)dK. numpy's FFT is in cyclic frequency without the
1/2π: `periodogram` converts and a Parseval test pins it. (b) "$\overline{u^2}$" in §12.6 is the variance of **one** component
(ē = (3/2)$\overline{u^2}$). (c) Skewness and kurtosis in §12.3 are un-normalised central moments (Exercise 12.1 calls them S and
K); the usual definitions divide by σ³ and σ⁴ — `statistics(normalized=)` gives both. (d) Reynolds numbers: Re_L = ΔU L/ν, R_λ
(with λ_f or λ_g — a factor √2), Re_x, Re_θ, Re_δ*, Re_d, δ⁺ = Re_τ — name the length and speed every time. (e) Wall variables:
y measured from the wall; channel between y = 0 and y = h, so the centreline is h/2 and δ = h/2 in the defect law. (f) Jet
similarity variable ξ = y/x (δ = x by convention, virtual origin dropped), profile widths are half-widths in ξ; wakes use
ξ = y/√(θ_p x) and r/(θ_r² x)^{1/3}.

**C5. Assumptions that switch silently.** Boussinesq with buoyancy in §12.5–12.7, constant density from §12.8 to §12.10 (ρ₀ → ρ,
P = deviation from hydrostatic), buoyancy back in §12.11. Ensemble averages throughout, but every measurement quoted uses time
averages (ergodicity is assumed, never named). The commutation of averaging with ∂/∂t is exact for ensemble averages only.
Homogeneity and isotropy in §12.6–12.7 are then applied to the small scales of shear flows (local isotropy). In §12.8 the
density difference between jet and ambient fluid is dropped downstream. The defect law assumes u_* is the outer velocity scale
(the book notes this is debated). §12.12 assumes stationary homogeneous turbulence with no mean flow, then applies it to a plume
in a wind (t = x/U).

**Risks for the implementation.**
- *Statistics are estimates.* Every assertion on sampled data needs a seed and a 5-standard-error tolerance; correlation tails
  are noisy (integrate to the first zero, state it); an Ornstein–Uhlenbeck signal has a cusp in r(τ) at 0, so its Taylor
  microscale does not exist — use a smooth-spectrum signal for (12.19) and say why.
- *Synthetic fields are not turbulence.* Random-phase fields have the prescribed spectrum and are solenoidal but have no energy
  cascade (zero skewness of velocity gradients). Label them "kinematic"; never claim dynamics from them.
- *Two- vs three-dimensional isotropy.* g = f + (r/2)f′ and ε̄ = 15ν⟨(∂u₁/∂x₁)²⟩ are three-dimensional results; a 2-D solenoidal
  field obeys g = d(rf)/dr and a factor 8 in place of 15 (to be derived and tested, not assumed). Tests of (12.41)–(12.43) need
  a 3-D field (64³ is enough, < 2 s).
- *Empirical constants.* κ (0.37–0.41), B, Π, the jet constants and the wake functions are data, with flow-dependent values:
  **no silent defaults** — `kappa` and `B` are required keywords or named presets with citations; the book's own nominal value
  in the ZPG fits and its worked example differ from the per-flow table (values private).
- *Rule 9.* The four ZPG fits, the two C_f laws, the Spalding and Coles forms, the log-linear profile and (12.92) are displayed
  equations and are shown as such (the first four without numbers in the book); Table 12.1's constants and half-widths, the
  per-flow (κ, B) pairs, the wake strength, the worked-example inputs and answers, the stirred-vessel and convection numbers and
  all exercise data are in the private JSON only. If the orchestrator prefers to treat the fitted coefficients of the ZPG
  formulas as "book-quoted numbers", move them to the JSON and reference Monkewitz et al. (2007) instead — a one-line decision.
- *Model results are not benchmarks.* The mixing-length channel, the jet energy budget and any k–ε run are "approximate" or
  "qualitative" against DNS; the plain κy mixing length without wall damping gives a log law with far too small an intercept —
  show it, with the damped version beside it.
- *Degenerate inputs for classifiers.* Rf at zero shear, L_M at zero heat flux, Ri at dU/dz = 0, the regime boundaries
  (Rf = 0, Rf_cr; y⁺ = 5, 30; t = Λ_t) — test both sides and the edge (ch11 lesson).
- *Cost.* Everything in §4 is closed-form or 1-D; the only heavy items are a 64³ synthetic field (< 2 s), 10⁴ Langevin
  particles (< 1 s) and the optional `k_epsilon_channel` (cache). Notebook budget is not at risk.
- *What the chapter does not give.* No DNS or LES is run here; `core.mac` could show a 2-D decaying field, but 2-D turbulence
  cascades energy upscale — if used, it must be labelled as the *opposite* of the 3-D cascade (a ch13 hook), never as an
  illustration of §12.7.
