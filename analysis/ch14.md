## 1. Chapter objectives and outline (our words; what is new vs restated from earlier chapters)

**What the chapter is for (paraphrasing the four objectives):**
1. Give the vocabulary of aircraft and wings (span, chord, planform, aspect ratio, camber, angle of attack, control
   surfaces, lift, drag, stall).
2. Work out what ideal flow predicts for a two-dimensional wing section: a shape from a conformal map, the circulation
   fixed by the trailing edge, and from it the lift.
3. Present the lifting-line theory of Prandtl and Lanchester for a wing of finite span: trailing vortices, downwash,
   induced drag, the elliptic loading.
4. Describe how fish, birds, insects and sails use lift for flight and for propulsion.

**Outline and what is new.**
- §14.1 Introduction — incompressible aerodynamics (low Mach number), three standing assumptions (no buoyancy, uniform
  constant-density stream, thin boundary layers), stall as the exception. *Restated*: ideal flow (ch06), boundary layers (ch09).
- §14.2 Aircraft terminology — fuselage, root, tip, span s, chord c, planform area A, aspect ratio (14.1)–(14.2), the
  three axes, control surfaces, trim. *New vocabulary*; no physics beyond L = W.
- §14.3 Characteristics of airfoil sections — section geometry, L and D, C_L and C_D (restated (4.107), (4.108)), C_p
  around a section, lift ⇔ circulation (restated (6.62)), **the Kutta condition**, stall, the impulsive start and the
  **starting vortex** (Kelvin's theorem, restated from ch05), historical notes. *New*: Kutta condition, starting vortex, stall.
- §14.4 Conformal transformation for generating airfoil shapes — thin-airfoil theory and Theodorsen's method named;
  the Zhukhovsky map (14.3) (restated (6.65)) applied to three circles: centred → flat plate; raised → circular arc
  (14.4)–(14.6); shifted → symmetric airfoil (14.7); both → cambered airfoil with the shape rules (14.8); the cusp.
  *New*: off-centre circles (ch06 mapped only centred ones).
- §14.5 Lift of a Zhukhovsky airfoil — velocity transformation (14.9), surface speed on the circle (14.10) (restated
  (6.37)), **Kutta circulation (14.11)**, **C_L ≃ 2π(α + β) (14.12)**, comparison with experiment. *New.*
- §14.6 Elementary lifting-line theory — tip flow and trailing vortex sheet, bound vortex, **downwash (14.13)**,
  effective angle (14.14), **lift and induced drag (14.15)**, the integral equation (14.16)–(14.17), the sine series
  (14.18)–(14.20), L and D_i from the coefficients (14.21)–(14.22), **the elliptic loading (14.23)–(14.26)**, the elliptic
  planform (14.27); Lanchester versus Prandtl. *New*; the filament formula is ch05 D13, Helmholtz ch05 C01.
- §14.7 Lift and drag characteristics — measured C_L(α) and C_D(α), stall, Reynolds-number dependence of maximum lift,
  camber and flaps, the drag breakdown (friction, form, induced, profile), sweepback and delta wings. *New*; separation
  and transition are ch09.
- §14.8 Propulsive mechanisms of fish and birds — an oscillating foil turns sideways work into thrust; flapping wings;
  a computed fruit-fly example quoted. *New.*
- §14.9 Sailing against the wind — apparent wind, the sail as a wing, the keel as a second wing, tacking. *New.*
- Exercises 14.1–14.19 — several *contain* results the text relies on: 14.2 (flow at a finite-angle trailing edge),
  14.3 (lift as the area of the C_p loop), 14.7 (leading-edge suction), 14.10 (the segment formula behind (14.13)),
  14.11 (horseshoe vortex), 14.15 (finite-wing lift slope).

**Mental model in one sentence:** "a wing lifts because it carries circulation; ideal flow allows any circulation, and
viscosity — acting only at the sharp trailing edge — picks the one value that lets the flow leave smoothly (Kutta), so
L = ρUΓ with Γ = 4πUa sin(α + β) gives C_L ≃ 2π(α + β); on a finite wing that circulation cannot end at the tips, so it
trails downstream as a vortex sheet whose downwash tilts the lift backward — an induced drag C_L²/(πΛ) that is the price
of lift and is smallest for an elliptic loading."

Spine for the curator (candidate sentence): **"lift is circulation; the trailing edge chooses it; the wing tips make you
pay for it."** Every section is one of those three clauses: §14.3–§14.5 the first two, §14.6–§14.7 the third,
§14.8–§14.9 the first clause used for thrust.

Hooks for a climate / geophysical reader (one clause each, for the curator): **starting vortex and Kelvin's theorem** —
the same material-circuit argument as the absolute-circulation and potential-vorticity arguments of ch05 and ch13;
**trailing vortices and downwash** — aircraft wake vortices, contrails and their distortion of cloud layers (the book's
cover), wake-turbulence separation at airports; **induced drag ∝ L²/(ρU²s²)** — why gliders, albatrosses and
high-altitude research aircraft have long thin wings, why wind-turbine blades are slender; **lift on a blade section** —
wind turbines and propellers are §14.5–§14.6 with a rotating relative wind; **formation flight** — migrating birds
flying in the upwash outboard of a neighbour's tip vortex; **sailing** — apparent wind, the same velocity triangle as
the relative wind on a turbine blade; **low-Reynolds-number flight** — insects, seeds and aerosols as the limit where
"thin boundary layers" fails.

### 9b. Traps (the page is right, the reader goes wrong)
- **T1 — Γ is clockwise.** As in ch06, the book's Γ in (6.62), (14.10), (14.11) is the clockwise circulation (loop
  circulation −Γ in the project's counterclockwise convention). Functions take `Gamma_cw=` / `Gamma_ccw=`; a positive
  lift at positive α needs Γ_cw > 0. The planted "read Γ as counterclockwise" variant must fail T7 and T11.
- **T2 — two different complex velocities.** dw/dz = u − iv here (ch06); ch13's Ekman V = u + iv. State it where (14.9)
  first appears.
- **T3 — b, a, β, c, e all change meaning or are overloaded.** b = map constant (chord ≃ 4b), but span in Exercises
  14.14, 14.18; a = circle radius (ch06: also the cylinder radius; Exercise 14.7: a length scale); **β = camber angle of
  the circle (§14.4–§14.5) = minus the zero-lift angle (§14.6–§14.7)** (and ch13's β = df/dy); c = chord (ch13: wave
  speed); e = thickness parameter (ours: span efficiency — code name `e_span`); Λ = aspect ratio (ch13: Rossby radius).
  γ = span angle in §14.6 (ch05: sheet strength; elsewhere the ratio of specific heats). A conventions table up front.
- **T4 — three θ's.** θ in §14.4 is the polar angle of ζ from the origin; θ in (14.10) is measured from the
  stream-aligned diameter through the circle's centre Q (ch06's convention for (6.37), tilted by α); θ in §14.8 and
  §14.9 is a velocity-triangle or course angle; θ₁, θ₂ in Exercise 14.10 are segment angles.
- **T5 — axes switch between sections.** §14.3–§14.5: section in the x–y plane, y up; in §14.4–§14.5 the x-axis is the
  mapped chord and the stream is inclined at α to it, so **lift is not F_y** (T7 asserts the force is normal to the
  stream). §14.6: y runs along the span and w is positive **downward**; Exercise 14.16 uses the opposite sign of w and
  says so; Exercises 14.17–14.18 take z up.
- **T6 — "the circulation does not change under the map" and "the velocities are equal at infinity" are stated, not
  shown** (D-m). The velocity is *not* equal anywhere else: (14.9) rescales it by ζ²/(ζ² − b²).
- **T7 — which circle parameters.** The book defines a = b(1 + e) only for the symmetric case and a = b/cos β only for
  the arc; Fig. 14.14 marks "a" as a horizontal distance. We use centre μ = b − ae^{−iβ}, a = b(1 + e)/cos β, which
  reduces to both. The exercise that plots a cambered airfoil gives a, b and β directly — the implementer must accept
  (a, b, β) as well as (b, e, β).
- **T8 — the trailing edge needs care numerically.** (14.9) is 0/0 at ζ = b with the Kutta circulation (finite limit,
  D-p) and infinite otherwise; the inverse map needs the outside root (ch06 lesson: numpy's principal root is wrong for
  Re z < 0), and for an off-centre circle "outside" means outside the *shifted* circle — choose the root by |ζ − μ| ≥ a,
  not by |ζ| ≥ b.
- **T9 — "induced drag is the largest part of the total drag".** Stated twice (pp. 713, 718). True where the lift
  coefficient is high (climb, best glide: exactly half at maximum L/D); at airliner cruise a cited figure is about 37 %
  with skin friction larger (§8). Teach it as a computed share that depends on speed (row 101), not as a rule.
- **T10 — the lifting line's downwash is infinite at the tips of a horseshoe and the bound vortex's own field is
  excluded.** (14.13) is a principal value; collocation points must avoid γ = 0, π; sin nγ/sin γ → n (times a sign) at
  the tips. The horseshoe's "better than elliptic" drag (Exercise 14.11) is an artefact of using the centre downwash for
  the whole span.
- **T11 — elliptic *loading* is not elliptic *planform*.** The optimum is a statement about Γ(y); a planform gives it
  only if untwisted with uniform sections, and twist can give it on any planform (Exercise 14.14). "Minimum induced
  drag" is for a fixed span and lift and a planar wake — the book says "only approximately optimal".
- **T12 — K.** The lift-curve slope is per **radian** in (14.16)–(14.20) and quoted per **degree** in §14.7; β in
  radians. K is a required keyword in LL functions (a default of 2π would silently encode "Zhukhovsky section").
- **T13 — A is the planform area** in (4.107)–(4.108) as used here and in (14.26); ch09 bluff bodies used the frontal
  area; a section uses the chord per unit span; Exercise 14.1 uses the frontal area; the fruit-fly coefficients use the
  wing surface.
- **T14 — the Kutta condition is a model of a viscous outcome, not a theorem of ideal flow**; it fails at stall and for
  unsteady motion faster than a chord-transit time; "Γ > Γ_Kutta is not observed" is an empirical statement.
- **T15 — Kelvin's theorem with viscosity inside the circuit**: only the circuit must stay in inviscid, constant-density
  fluid (ch05's hypotheses); the foil and its boundary layers are inside. The circuit is material, so it must be large
  enough that the wake never crosses it during the time considered.
- **T16 — rows 19, 54 (roll-up), 96, 98 are qualitative models of ours**, not book formulas: laminar Thwaites on an
  attached ideal pressure distribution cannot predict maximum lift; the generic curves are drawn, not derived. Label
  them "qualitative" and list them in the verification report's Open items.
- **T17 — rule 9.** Typical values the book quotes in words (the stall angles, the lift-slope per degree, the camber
  percentage, the limiting sailing angle, the Reynolds-number ranges, the fruit-fly coefficients, the aircraft mass) are
  private; the exercise inputs and answers are private; "illustrative" notebook inputs must be visibly different.
  Standard public constants (2π, the low-Mach limit, 3√3/4) are not book values.
- **T18 — fish and sail formulas are quasi-steady and drag-free in the book.** The "ideally perfect" conversion is an
  identity of the velocity triangle, not an efficiency prediction; the limiting sailing angle is set by the two
  lift-to-drag ratios (ours), which the book does not derive.
