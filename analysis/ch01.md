## 1. Chapter objectives and outline (our words)

**What the book says the chapter is for (paraphrased from the chapter objectives):**
1. Explain what fluid mechanics is and why it matters.
2. Say which assumptions the whole subject rests on, especially the continuum idea.
3. Review the basic science of liquids and gases: molecular transport, surface tension, thermodynamics, the perfect gas.
4. Give the parts of fluid statics used later: isotropic pressure, hydrostatics, and the stability of a stratified fluid.
5. Set up dimensional analysis (Buckingham's Π theorem) as a thinking tool for the rest of the book.

**Outline (11 sections + exercises):**
1.1 fluid mechanics (prose) · 1.2 SI units · 1.3 how solids, liquids and gases differ · 1.4 continuum hypothesis and the
Knudsen number · 1.5 molecular transport: Fick, Fourier and Newton laws, dynamic and kinematic viscosity · 1.6 surface
tension and the Laplace pressure jump (Example 1.1: capillary rise) · 1.7 fluid statics: pressure is the same in every
direction, Pascal's law, the hydrostatic law · 1.8 classical thermodynamics: fluid particle, first law, equations of
state, enthalpy, specific heats, second law and entropy, Gibbs relations, speed of sound, thermal expansion ·
1.9 perfect gas: the molecular and continuum gas laws, R = Cp − Cv, γ, isentropic relations, c = √(γRT), α = 1/T ·
1.10 static stability: displaced-parcel argument and Brunt–Väisälä frequency N, adiabatic lapse rate, potential
temperature and potential density, the ocean criterion, isothermal atmosphere and scale height · 1.11 dimensional
analysis: dimensional homogeneity, Buckingham Π, the 7-step procedure on pipe pressure drop, Examples 1.2 (scale height),
1.3 (Pythagoras), 1.4 (G. I. Taylor's blast wave), 1.5 (Rayleigh scattering) · 30 exercises · literature.

**New vs restated.** Chapter 1 is the start of the book, so nearly everything is **NEW** here. The only items that
really come from before the book are SI units, prefixes and the Celsius–kelvin offset (1.2). They are marked SEEN
(pre-book). The book says it only *reviews* thermodynamics (1.8), but for this project first law, entropy, Gibbs
relations, specific heats and Maxwell-relation arguments go beyond school physics, so they count as NEW.
Pre-book prerequisites that are used without being stated are listed per row: Newton's second law, Archimedes'
buoyancy, derivatives and partial derivatives, Taylor series to first order, integrals, logarithms, vectors and
gradients, determinants/matrix rank, and linear systems.

**Content deferred to later chapters (pointers in the text):** tensor form of Newton's viscosity law (Ch. 4), pressure in
a moving fluid (Ch. 4), surface tension as a boundary condition (end of Ch. 4), proof of c² = (∂p/∂ρ)_s (Ch. 15),
second-law implications (Ch. 4), named dimensionless groups (§4.11), Bernoulli (§4.9).

## 9. Risks, conventions, ambiguities, suspected typos

**Sign and orientation conventions**
1. **Lapse rate sign.** The book defines $\Gamma\equiv dT/dz$, so $\Gamma_a=-g\alpha T/C_p$ is *negative* (the book's rounded value is in the JSON).
   Meteorology (AMS) usually defines the lapse rate as $-dT/dz$ (positive 9.8 K/km). Code names must say which:
   `adiabatic_lapse_rate` returns dT/dz (negative), and the docstring says so. Tests compare magnitudes with AMS.
2. **z is upward** in §§1.7 and 1.10 (p₀ at z = 0, depth h = −z). Record this in `knowledge/notation.md`. Chapter 13 and
   oceanography often use depth-positive coordinates.
3. **Contact angle in Ex. 1.1.** The book's α is measured so that the vertical tension component is $\sigma\sin\alpha$,
   which makes α the complement of the usual contact angle $\theta_c$ ($\sin\alpha=\cos\theta_c$). So α = 90° means
   full wetting, which matches the exercise's "wetting angle nearly 90°". `capillary_rise` takes the book's α and
   the docstring gives the conversion.
4. **Laplace jump:** $p_i$ is on the concave side. For saddle surfaces the radii are signed. The book handles only
   positive radii.
5. **First-law signs:** $\delta w$ is work done *on* the system (positive in), so reversible work is $-p\,dv$.

**Symbol collisions (flag for `notation.md`)**
α is both the contact angle (Ex. 1.1) and the thermal expansion coefficient (1.20). θ is the wedge angle (Fig. 1.5),
the temperature dimension (§1.11) and the potential temperature (1.31). T is both temperature and the time dimension.
h is capillary height and enthalpy. R is a radius, the gas constant and the principal radii R₁, R₂. σ is surface
tension (later stress). s is entropy; S is salinity (1.10) and scattered intensity (Ex. 1.5). c is sound speed and an
exponent. d is diameter, distance and the differential. n is the number of molecules and the number of variables.
k is conductivity while $k_B$ is Boltzmann's constant. **Subscripts o and 0:** $p_o$ is the outer pressure in (1.5),
$p_0$ is the pressure at z = 0 in (1.9), and $p_o$ is the standard reference pressure in (1.31), which the book also
equates with p(0). In code the reference pressure is `p_ref` (default 1.0e5 Pa), kept separate from surface
pressure.

**Silently switching assumptions**
- (1.11), (1.17) hold for reversible processes, while (1.18) holds for any process. The book explains this, but the
  notebook must keep the distinction visible.
- (1.25)–(1.28) need a perfect gas with **constant** specific heats, yet the book notes just before that $C_p$ and $C_v$
  vary with T.
- (1.29) describes an isentropic parcel in any fluid, (1.31)–(1.34) are for a perfect gas only, and (1.35) is for
  seawater at fixed salinity with $\rho_a\cong\rho$.
- The scale-height section switches to an isothermal atmosphere, justified by a rough temperature band over the lower
  atmosphere (numbers in JSON). The verifier should compare that band with the USSA-1976 table (to be stored in
  `reference/ch01/`) and report it as an order-of-magnitude statement, not a precise one.
- The book says that for N² > 0 "viscous forces and thermal conduction will arrest any oscillatory motion", but the
  parcel ODE has no damping, so its solution oscillates forever at frequency N. The explainer should show the undamped
  oscillation and treat damping as outside the model.
- Example 1.4: K comes from Taylor's full similarity solution. It depends on γ and on spherical vs hemispherical
  geometry (Fig. 1.11 draws a hemisphere on the ground, while Taylor's 0.856 is for a free spherical blast). Treat K as
  an input and document it.

**Equations stated without derivation** (all written out in §2b): (1.5) general Laplace (D02); (1.19) (proof deferred to
Ch. 15); (1.23) (D12); the e(T), h(T) claim (D13, Exercise 1.10); (1.25) (D14, Exercise 1.11); (1.26) (D15);
(1.27)–(1.28) (D16–D17); parcel equation and (1.29) (D18, Exercise 1.13); (1.30) (D19, Exercise 1.14, needs a Maxwell
relation, which the chapter never introduces, so a primer is needed); Buckingham's theorem (1.37) (D28).

**Suspected typos (book prints X, we believe Y)**
1. **Fig. 1.2 caption:** "the mass fraction profile increases with increasing *Y*". It should say increasing *y*, since
   the profile is Y(y).
2. **§1.9, after (1.26):** "(see Exercise 1.8)". Exercise 1.8 is the capillary-rise problem. The isentropic relations
   are Exercise 1.11, which the text cites correctly one paragraph earlier, so Y = "Exercise 1.11".
3. **§1.11 Step 5 (inspection method):** twice refers to "(1.32)" for the dimensional matrix. It should be "(1.39)",
   because (1.32) is the potential-temperature equation.
4. **Clausius–Duhem inequality (§1.8 ii):** printed as $s_2-s_1\ge\int dq_{\rm rev}/T$. For an arbitrary process the
   integrand must be the *actual* heat $\delta q$ divided by the boundary temperature, since with $dq_{\rm rev}$ the
   right side equals $s_2-s_1$ by (1.16). We implement and teach $s_2-s_1\ge\int\delta q/T$.
5. **§1.8 heat and specific heats:** $(\partial Q/\partial T)_v$ uses a capital Q for a per-unit-mass quantity, against
   the book's own lowercase convention, and a partial derivative of a path function is not well defined. Read it as
   $\delta q/dT$ along the stated reversible process.
6. **§1.2:** "°C = K − 273.15" looks like a relation between units. It means $T_{[^\circ C]}=T_{[K]}-273.15$.
7. **(1.35):** $d\rho_\theta/dz=d\rho/dz-d\rho_a/dz$ is written as an equality. From (1.33) for a perfect gas,
   $d\rho_\theta/dz=(p_o/p)^{1/\gamma}(d\rho/dz+\rho g/c^2)$, so the equality holds only up to a positive factor that
   is ≈ 1 near the reference pressure. The **sign**, which is all the stability criterion uses, is correct. Teach it as
   "has the same sign as".
8. **Ex. 1.5 step 3:** the rank-2 reason given is "all dimensions are either intensity or length". The algebraic reason
   is that the T row is −3 times the M row. Not an error, but the notebook should show the row dependence.
9. **Mean free path:** the book's value (JSON) sits inside the published spread (38.5–67.3 nm) but below the widely used
   Jennings 67.3 nm. Label it an order-of-magnitude value.
10. **Gas viscosity ∝ $T^{1/2}$:** this is only rough. Against Sutherland/USSA it overestimates μ at 216.65 K by about 9 %.
    Expose the exponent as a parameter and show the comparison.
11. **Constants:** the book's $k_B$ and $A_o$ are rounded pre-2019 values, and its $R_u$, R and $C_p$ are rounded.
    Differences from CODATA/USSA are 0.02–0.3 %. V6 book-value tests use ≤ 0.5 % or report the difference. They must
    not tighten against the book.

**Garbled-extraction hazards found (image resolved all of them)**
Minus signs dropped in (1.1), (1.2), (1.8), (1.9), (1.20), (1.29), (1.30), (1.34) and in the determinant value −1. The
text shows "1" for −1 and loses the matrix (1.39) and the Ex. 1.2–1.5 matrices entirely, so they were read from the
images. "γ" prints as "g", "θ" as "q", "τ" as "s", "σ" as "s", "ν" as "n", "ε" as "3", "≡" as "h", "≅" as "y", ζ is
dropped. The exponent algebra of Ex. 1.2 was re-solved with sympy (a = −1, b = 1, c = 1, d = −1) and agrees with the
image. No `?SYM?` remains.

**Numerical / implementation risks**
- pint offset temperatures (`degC`) are not multiplicative. Use kelvin inside functions, and `delta_degC` for
  differences.
- pint gives kmol the dimension [substance]. Ex. 1.2 drops it. Since the [substance] row equals −1 × the M row for
  $M_w$ and $R_u$, dropping it does not change the rank; `dimension_vector` should drop it and say so.
- `pi_groups`: a basis is not unique. Tests must check the properties (dimensionless, independent, count n − r), plus
  exact agreement with the book only when the book's repeating set is passed.
- `np.gradient` is first order at the edges. Use `edge_order=2` or write the stencils out when measuring order (F47).
- FTCS diffusion is our choice, not the book's. State it in the docstring and enforce the stability limit.
- `parcel_ode` with N² < 0 grows exponentially. Cap the integration time or stop with an event.
