## 1. Chapter objectives and outline (our words)

**What the book says the chapter is for (paraphrased from the chapter objectives):**
1. Fix the notation for scalars, vectors and tensors used in the rest of the book (boldface and index notation, the summation convention).
2. Review the algebra of vectors and matrices: rotation of axes, matrix products, contraction.
3. Show how vector differentiation (gradient, divergence, curl) acts on scalars, vectors and tensors.
4. Review the two integral theorems of vector field theory (Gauss, Stokes) and the integral definitions of field derivatives they imply.

**Outline (14 sections + exercises):**
2.1 scalars, vectors, second-order tensors; summation convention (2.1)–(2.2) · 2.2 rotation of axes, direction cosines, the formal definition of a vector (2.3)–(2.8), Example 2.1 (Cartesian → polar) · 2.3 matrix inner product (2.9)–(2.11) · 2.4 second-order tensors, the stress tensor and its sign convention, transformation rule (2.12), higher order (2.13) · 2.5 contraction, invariants, products (2.14) · 2.6 force on a surface: Cauchy's traction formula (2.15), Example 2.2 (traction in a shear flow) · 2.7 Kronecker delta and alternating tensor (2.16)–(2.19) · 2.8 dot and cross products (2.20)–(2.21) · 2.9 ∇, gradient, divergence, curl (2.22)–(2.25), Example 2.3 · 2.10 symmetric and antisymmetric parts, the vector ↔ antisymmetric-tensor map (2.26)–(2.29) · 2.11 eigenvalues and eigenvectors of a symmetric tensor, Example 2.4 (principal axes of a shear strain rate) · 2.12 Gauss' theorem and the integral definitions of ∇, ∇·, ∇× (2.30)–(2.33), Example 2.5 · 2.13 Stokes' theorem and circulation (2.34)–(2.35), Example 2.6 · 2.14 comma notation (2.36) · 20 exercises · literature.

**New vs restated.** The chapter is a mathematics review, so much of the *content* is pre-book (vectors, dot and cross product, matrix multiplication, eigenvalues — the book says the reader "is assumed to be familiar" with eigenvalues). What is genuinely **NEW** for this project is the *tensor point of view*: the summation convention, the direction-cosine matrix and the transformation rules (2.5), (2.8), (2.12), (2.13) as the *definition* of a vector/tensor, the stress tensor and Cauchy's formula (2.15), δ_ij and ε_ijk with the epsilon–delta identity, the symmetric/antisymmetric split and its vector, the principal-axis interpretation, and the integral definitions of ∇, ∇·, ∇× via Gauss and Stokes. **SEEN** rows point to ch01 (stress traction §1.3 `C03`, τ_xy = μ∂u/∂y §1.5 `C12`, Newton's third law inside D05, the ∇/partial-derivative/Taylor primers) or to pre-book linear algebra (marked "pre-book").

**Content deferred to later chapters (pointers in the text):** symmetry of the stress tensor τ_ij = τ_ji (Ch. 4); why curl-free is "irrotational" and R as the rotation tensor of the vorticity ω (Ch. 3); the strain-rate interpretation of Example 2.4 (Ch. 3); comma notation used in §5.6; polar unit vectors (Fig. 3.3a).

## 9. Risks, conventions, ambiguities, suspected typos

**Sign, index and orientation conventions (record in `knowledge/notation.md`)**
1. **Direction-cosine matrix.** $C_{ij}=\mathbf e_i\cdot\mathbf e'_j$: row = old axis, column = new axis; the *columns* of C are the new unit vectors written in old components. Components transform **passively**: $\mathbf x'=\mathbf C^{\rm T}\mathbf x$, $\mathbf x=\mathbf C\mathbf x'$, $\boldsymbol\tau'=\mathbf C^{\rm T}\boldsymbol\tau\mathbf C$. Wikipedia's R(θ) and `scipy Rotation.as_matrix()` are **active** matrices (they rotate the vector); the book's C for a frame rotated by +θ *equals* R(θ) but is applied as Rᵀ. Code: `rotation_matrix_2d/3d` return C and every docstring says "passive: columns = new axes". Test: Ex. 2.1 C = [[cos, −sin],[sin, cos]] and x' = Cᵀx gives (u_r, u_θ).
2. **Traction contracts the first index**: $f_i=\tau_{ji}n_j$ (row of τ = face normal). Wikipedia writes the same ($T_j=\sigma_{ij}n_i$). $\mathbf f=\mathbf n\cdot\boldsymbol\tau$ equals $\boldsymbol\tau\cdot\mathbf n$ only when τ is symmetric — which Ch. 4 proves, but code must not assume it: `traction` uses `einsum('ji,j->i')` and a test with a non-symmetric τ proves the distinction.
3. **Divergence of a tensor contracts the second index**: $(\nabla\cdot\boldsymbol\tau)_i=\partial\tau_{ij}/\partial x_j$ — the opposite index from (2.15). Consistent only for symmetric τ (Ch. 4 momentum equation uses $\partial\tau_{ij}/\partial x_j$). `tensor_divergence(T, h, index=1)` exposes the choice.
4. **Velocity-gradient index order.** §2.9 says the gradient raises the order "i.e. ∂u_i/∂x_j", while (2.30)–(2.31) place the derivative index first ($\partial Q_j/\partial x_i\leftrightarrow n_iQ_j$). Fix once: `vector_gradient(u)[i, j] = ∂u_i/∂x_j` (Kundu's Ch. 3 usage, $S_{ij}=\tfrac12(\partial_ju_i+\partial_iu_j)$); the antisymmetric part's sign depends on this — the ch03 rotation tensor test must pin it (F19: `antisymmetric_part(vector_gradient(b×x))` ↔ vector b, i.e. ω = ∇×u = 2b).
5. **Double dot.** The book's $\mathbf A:\mathbf B=A_{ij}B_{ji}$ (transpose pairing) differs from the common $A_{ij}B_{ij}$ (Frobenius; Wikipedia/Gurtin). Both coincide for symmetric operands (the fluid-mechanics use in Ch. 4). `double_dot(A, B, convention="book"|"frobenius")`, no silent default in the notebook text: state the convention where used.
6. **Stress sign**: tensile positive; on the face with outward normal $+\mathbf e_i$ the positive components point along $+\mathbf e_j$; on the opposite face they reverse. Pressure will enter as $\tau_{ij}=-p\delta_{ij}+\dots$ in Ch. 4 (ch01's isotropic p pushes *inward* = negative normal stress). ch01's signed $\tau_{xy}=\mu\,\partial u/\partial y$ is $\tau_{21}=\tau_{12}$ here.
7. **Stokes orientation**: choose the outside of A → n; $\mathbf t=\mathbf n_c\times\mathbf n$ with $\mathbf n_c$ the outward (in-surface) normal to C; t is counterclockwise seen from the outside (right-hand rule between n and t). Flipping n flips both sides of (2.34). `planar_loop` builds t from `normal` and asserts $\oint(\mathbf x-\mathbf c)\times\mathbf t\,ds=2A\mathbf n$.
8. **Grid layout** (new convention for the whole project): 3-D arrays are `[k, j, i]` = (z, y, x), x on the last axis, built with `np.meshgrid(z, y, x, indexing='ij')`; 2-D `[j, i]` with `indexing='xy'` (`math-to-python` §3). Vector fields stack the component on axis 0: `u[c, k, j, i]`. Any deviation later will silently transpose curls.

**Symbol collisions (flag for `notation.md`)**
- **Γ** = shear rate $du_1/dx_2$ in Ex. 2.4 (code `Gamma_shear`), vs ch01 lapse rate Γ = dT/dz, vs circulation Γ in Ch. 5–6 (the book writes the right side of (2.34) as "circulation" without a symbol here). Three meanings; code names must differ (`Gamma_shear`, `dT_dz`, `circulation`).
- **ω** = the vector associated with R (vorticity in Ch. 3); ω = angular frequency in Ch. 7. τ = stress tensor here vs ch01 pendulum period preset. **S** = strain-rate tensor (Ex. 2.4) vs ch01 salinity/scattered intensity. **A** = antisymmetric part (§2.10) *and* the surface area (§2.12–2.13) *and* a generic tensor (§2.4–2.5) *and* ch01 dimensional matrix. **R** = antisymmetric/rotation tensor vs ch01 gas constant/radius. **C** = direction-cosine matrix vs ch01 Pythagoras hypotenuse preset; **P** = matrix product / doubly contracted scalar. **Q** = generic field in §2.12 vs ch01 heat. **λ^k** = eigenvalue with a superscript *label* (not a power) — code `lam[k]`. **b^k** eigenvectors vs **b** the constant vector of Ex. 2.3. **f** = traction (force per area), f = |f|. **δ** = Kronecker delta (also the virtual-displacement/inexact-differential δ of ch01 §1.8). **a** = the shear stress value in Ex. 2.2 and the scalar in Ex. 2.3.
- **Coordinates**: §2.9–2.12 use $x_1,x_2,x_3$; Ex. 2.6 switches to $(x,y,z)$ and $(u_x,u_y,u_z)$ without comment.

**Silently switching assumptions**
- (2.15) is derived for a general τ, but "$\mathbf f=\mathbf n\cdot\boldsymbol\tau$" (boldface) is stated to need τ_ij = τ_ji; (2.12) is *stated* via a tetrahedron balance (Sommerfeld) that in fact needs (2.15) — our D06 route reverses the book's order (2.12 → 2.15) into 2.15 → 2.12; the notebook must say so.
- §2.11 assumes a **real symmetric** tensor throughout; facts (1)–(4) fail for non-symmetric tensors (complex eigenvalues, non-orthogonal eigenvectors). `principal_axes` must raise for non-symmetric input rather than call `eigh` silently.
- Fact (2) is stated for *distinct* eigenvalues; for repeated eigenvalues orthogonal eigenvectors still exist but are not unique — `eigh` returns one valid choice; tests must compare invariantly (projectors), not eigenvector by eigenvector.
- Fact (4) says the λ^k bound "the elements τ_ij"; precisely, the **normal** stresses $n_i\tau_{ij}n_j$ lie in $[\lambda_{\min},\lambda_{\max}]$ and the shear magnitude is bounded by $(\lambda_{\max}-\lambda_{\min})/2$ (Mohr). An off-diagonal element $\tau'_{12}$ in some frame can exceed |λ_min| in magnitude only if... it cannot exceed $(\lambda_{\max}-\lambda_{\min})/2$, which is ≤ max|λ|; so the loose statement is true for elements but the sharp version is the useful one — teach the sharp version.
- "δ_ij is the only isotropic tensor of second order" and "only one of third order" hold **up to a scalar multiple** and, for ε, only under **proper** rotations (det C = +1): ε changes sign under a reflection (pseudotensor). Under the book's "rotation of axes" (det +1) the statement is right; `is_isotropic(..., proper=True)` is the default and the notebook says why.
- The integral definitions (2.31)–(2.33) presume the limit exists and is shape-independent (continuity of the derivatives); the "recipes in any coordinate system" remark (Exercises 2.16–2.18) needs the coordinate-dependent variation of the unit vectors — deferred to ch03/App. B.
- Comma notation (2.36) equals the partial derivative only in Cartesian coordinates (no Christoffel terms); the book uses it only there.
