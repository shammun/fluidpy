## 1. Chapter objectives and outline (our words; what is new vs restated from earlier chapters)

**The chapter's stated objectives (gist, our words).** (i) Show how velocity, density, pressure and temperature act on each
other once pressure changes are large enough to change the density. (ii) Describe isentropic flow in a duct whose area
changes slowly. (iii) Obtain the jumps across a normal shock from the conservation laws. (iv) Describe what wall friction
and heat exchange do to a compressible flow in a duct of constant area. (v) Show how the shape of a wall sends out pressure
waves that compress, expand and turn a steady supersonic stream.

**Spine (proposed, one sentence the curator may sharpen).** *Every result in the chapter is the same three conservation
laws (mass, momentum, energy) plus the perfect-gas law, written between two stations and expressed through one number, the
Mach number; the factor 1 − M² that appears in every answer changes sign at the speed of sound, and that sign change is why
supersonic flow does the opposite of what incompressible intuition expects.*

**Outline (what each section does; NEW vs restated).**
- **§15.1 Introduction** — restated: Mach number (4.111), sound speed (1.19), the M² scaling of ∇·u (4.110), the M < 0.3
  rule (ch04, ch14), the perfect-gas relations of §1.8–§1.9 collected as (15.1)–(15.3). New: the five flow regimes by Mach
  number; the three "surprises" the chapter explains.
- **§15.2 Acoustics** — new: sound as the linearised theory; p′ ≅ c²ρ′; linearised continuity and Euler equations about a
  uniform stream; the convected wave equation (15.12); d'Alembert's solution (15.15); the particle velocity (15.16); waves
  in a moving stream (15.17) and the first appearance of "nothing travels upstream when U > c"; nonlinear steepening as the
  road to shocks. Restated: c² = γRT (1.27).
- **§15.3 Basic equations for one-dimensional flow** — control-volume mass, energy and momentum for a slowly varying duct:
  (15.18)–(15.26). New in this form; all three are ch04 integral laws ((4.5), (4.48), (4.17)) applied to one control volume.
- **§15.4 Reference properties** — new: the stagnation state (isentropic deceleration to rest) and its ratios
  (15.28)–(15.30); constancy of T₀ in adiabatic flow and loss of p₀ with entropy; the sonic state and the area ratio (15.31);
  Table 15.1.
- **§15.5 Area–velocity relationship** — new: (15.32)–(15.34), nozzles and diffusers in both regimes, the throat, the
  convergent–divergent nozzle; Example 15.1.
- **§15.6 Normal shock waves** — new: jump conditions (15.35)–(15.38), the Rankine–Hugoniot relations (15.39)–(15.42), the
  entropy jump (15.43) and its cubic start (15.44a, b), the second-law selection M₁ > 1, the moving shock by a Galilean
  shift, shock structure from the steady 1-D Navier–Stokes equations (Becker's integral), Table 15.2.
- **§15.7 Nozzles at different back pressures** — new: choking of a convergent nozzle; the regimes of a Laval nozzle
  (subsonic, shock inside, shock at the exit, over-expanded, design, under-expanded); Example 15.2. No numbered equation.
- **§15.8 Friction and heating in constant-area ducts** — new: one set of jump relations with a friction parameter f and a
  heat parameter q (15.45), their closed solution (15.46), the differential forms (15.47), (15.48), choking by friction or
  heat. The book never uses the names Fanno and Rayleigh; the chapter blurb in `book.yaml` does.
- **§15.9 Pressure waves in planar flow** — new: Mach cone and angle (15.49), oblique shocks (15.50)–(15.53) with weak and
  strong branches and a maximum deflection, the weak-wave rule (15.54)–(15.55), the Prandtl–Meyer fan and function (15.56).
- **§15.10 Thin airfoil theory in supersonic flow** — new: Ackeret's linear theory on a flat plate, (15.57)–(15.59), wave
  drag. Compared with (14.12) of ch14.
- **Exercises 15.1–15.18** — eleven of them complete derivations the text leaves out ((15.15), (15.16), (15.31), (15.44),
  (15.47), (15.48), the choked mass flow, the choking limits, tangential momentum across an oblique shock, the general thin
  airfoil, the strong-shock limit); they are inventoried where they feed a row.

### 9b. Traps (the page is right, the reader goes wrong)
- **T1 — subscript 0 means three things.** Reference values of the scaling (4.110) in §15.1; the undisturbed state of the
  acoustic split (15.4) in §15.2 (T₀ is then just the local temperature); the stagnation state from §15.4 on. In a moving
  stream the acoustic p₀ and the stagnation p₀ differ. The notebook needs a callout where (15.4) appears and again at h₀.
- **T2 — three different stars and an A that is not an area.** A* is the control surface in (15.20), (15.23); the sonic
  throat area from (15.31) on; and "A" in (15.46) is a pure number. The starred states of the friction and heating
  literature (sonic state of the *same* frictional or heated process) are a third meaning that the book does not use —
  our `fanno_ratios` / `rayleigh_ratios` must say so in every docstring and never be mixed with A/A*.
- **T3 — regime boundaries are nominal.** The transonic band and especially the hypersonic threshold are conventions (the
  book's hypersonic threshold is lower than the customary one; book value, private). `mach_regime` takes them as keywords.
- **T4 — γ and the gas constants.** Everything assumes constant γ; both tables and Figs. 15.8, 15.9, 15.15, 15.16, 15.19 are
  for γ = 1.4 only. The rounded constants of (15.3) are not mutually exact (C_p/C_v = 1.4003, C_p − C_v = R exactly), and
  the project's `R_AIR`, `CP_AIR` differ in the fourth digit — a book number is reproduced with the book's constants
  passed explicitly. Example 15.1 uses a different R for the combustion gas with γ = 1.4.
- **T5 — letters reused.** S is entropy per unit mass (s in ch01); e internal energy; h enthalpy (a depth in ch07, ch13);
  Q heat added per unit mass, q the *dimensionless* heat Q/h₁, q_j the heat-flux vector; f, g d'Alembert's two functions
  in §15.2 but f the dimensionless friction F/(p₁A) in §15.8 (and neither is the Darcy or Fanning friction factor); F
  three ways (T11); U the scaled speed u/V inside the shock, V a constant of integration there but V₁, V₂ flow speeds at an
  oblique shock, v the shock-parallel speed; η the stretched coordinate; μ the Mach angle *and* the viscosity; ν the
  Prandtl–Meyer function (kinematic viscosity on p. 753; the glyph prints like v on p. 773); δ the deflection angle *and*
  the shock thickness; σ the shock angle (growth rate in ch11); b the chord (span symbol s and map constant b in ch14); c
  the sound speed (chord in ch14); m the mass flux inside the shock but ṁ the mass flow of the duct. A letter table "here /
  elsewhere" up front, as in ch14.
- **T6 — which Mach number is upstream.** Station 1 is always upstream, and (15.40) is its own inverse, so feeding the
  downstream value in as M₁ returns the upstream one *without any error* — the expansion shock. The NASA page numbers the
  stations 0 and 1. `normal_shock` refuses M₁ < 1 unless asked.
- **T7 — stagnation quantities depend on the frame.** Across a stationary shock T₀ is unchanged; across the same shock
  seen from the still gas the gas behind has been set in motion and heated, and its T₀ in that frame is higher than ahead.
  Static p, ρ, T, c and S do not depend on the frame; u, M, T₀, p₀ do.
- **T8 — the area ratio has two inverses, and A* is not conserved across a shock.** Downstream of a shock the subsonic
  branch applies and A* has grown by p₀₁/p₀₂ (Example 15.2 turns on this).
- **T9 — silent assumptions of Example 15.1.** The chamber state is taken as the stagnation state and the exit pressure
  as the ambient pressure, so the pressure-thrust term is zero; off design it is not.
- **T10 — "choked" does not mean the mass flow cannot be raised.** It cannot be raised by lowering the back pressure;
  raising p₀ (or lowering T₀) still raises it. And the exit-plane pressure equals the back pressure only when the exit is
  subsonic.
- **T11 — the sign and meaning of the wall force.** F in (15.24) is the force of the walls *on the fluid* in +x; F_f in
  (15.25) is a positive retarding force per unit length; F in f = F/(p₁A) of (15.45) is the total retarding force (f > 0).
  The book's f lumps the whole duct into one number referred to the *inlet* pressure; it is not 4fL/D, and two ducts in
  series do not add their f. The differential forms (15.47), (15.48) hold at the station where p = p₁, T = T₁.
- **T12 — which root of (15.46).** The root that continues M₂ = M₁ as f, q → 0 is the "+" sign for M₁ < 1 and the "−" sign
  for M₁ > 1 (analyst check at M₁ = 0.5 and 2); the other root is the state behind a normal shock (or the excluded
  supersonic state). The denominator vanishes at 2Aγ² = γ − 1, where the numerator vanishes too. No real root means choked.
- **T13 — oblique-shock conventions.** σ and δ are both measured from the upstream flow direction; most sources call them
  β and θ. "Weak" and "strong" name the two roots, not the weak-wave limit of (15.55): a "weak" root at 20° deflection is a
  strong compression. The flow behind a weak root is not always supersonic (a narrow band below δ_max). M₂ needs the
  division by sin(σ − δ). A wedge of half-angle above about 45.6° (γ = 1.4) can never hold an attached shock.
- **T14 — the sign of the turning angle.** In (15.55), (15.57) and in δ + ν(M) = const, δ is positive when the wall turns
  *into* the flow (compression); an expansion is a negative δ and increases ν. Our `prandtl_meyer_turn(turn=)` takes the
  expansion as positive and says so. The book's "first quadrant" and "fourth quadrant" remarks are about which family of
  Mach lines carries the wave.
- **T15 — §15.10 geometry.** b is the chord; the plate is tilted nose-up by α, so the upper surface turns away from the
  flow (expansion, p₂ < p_∞) and the lower into it (p₃ > p_∞); L and D use cos α ≈ 1 and sin α ≈ α; coefficients are per
  unit span on the chord. Lift comes with circulation but without a Kutta condition.
- **T16 — how small is "small"?** For M₁ = 2 the exact oblique-shock pressure rise exceeds the linear rule (15.55) by 1 % at half a
  degree and 12 % at 5°, and at 20° the linear rule is about 39 % low (analyst); it fails as M → 1 (the coefficient diverges) and near
  δ_max. Every use of (15.55)–(15.59) should print the exact shock–expansion value beside it.
- **T17 — adiabatic is not isentropic.** T₀ survives friction and shocks; p₀ does not. The Bernoulli equation (constant
  h₀ *and* constant entropy) cannot be carried across a shock, although (15.37) looks like it.
- **T18 — the two "paradoxes" are regime statements.** Friction accelerates only a subsonic duct flow; heating lowers the
  temperature only for 1/√γ < M < 1 (the book writes the band for M²). Entropy rises in both; nothing is violated.
- **T19 — acoustics.** (15.14) is an indefinite time integral (the constant is fixed by a fluid at rest before the wave);
  the sign of u′ for the left-going part is negative for a compression; "downstream" in (15.17) presumes U > 0; the
  particle velocity is not the wave speed (u′/c equals the condensation).
- **T20 — shock structure.** Pr″ is built on μ″ = (4/3)μ + μ_v, not on μ (for air with μ_v = 0 it is about 0.95, which is
  why Pr″ = 1 is reasonable); η is only roughly a count of mean free paths, and its origin is arbitrary; the continuum
  model is being used at its limit, and the book restricts the result to moderate Mach numbers.
- **T21 — the hydraulic-jump analogy is partial.** The jump dissipates mechanical energy and conserves mass and momentum;
  the shock conserves mass, momentum *and* energy and loses stagnation pressure. The quantitative analogy (γ = 2) holds
  for the isentropic parts only.
- **T22 — Exercise 15.9.** The blast is hemispherical (half the volume of a sphere); the constant quoted from the
  literature is for that exercise's normalisation and is not the constant stored in `reference/ch01/`.
