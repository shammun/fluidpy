## 1. Chapter objectives and outline (our words)

**What the chapter is for (paraphrasing the three chapter objectives):**
1. Say precisely what "ideal flow" is — constant density plus irrotational — which equations it keeps, which boundary condition it drops (no-slip), and where in a real flow it can be trusted (outside thin, attached boundary layers; upstream of separation) and where it cannot (wakes, pipes, turbulence, strong stratification, high Mach number).
2. Show how two scalar functions — the stream function ψ and the velocity potential φ, later fused into one complex potential w = φ + iψ — turn the nonlinear flow problem into a *linear* Laplace problem, in plane (2-D), axisymmetric and fully 3-D flows; pressure then follows from Bernoulli.
3. Build and use the classical solutions: flows past a half-body, a circular cylinder with and without circulation, a sphere and an airship-like body; the forces they predict (zero drag — d'Alembert — and lift L = ρUΓ — Kutta–Zhukhovsky); how to get new flows by conformal mapping and by numerical methods; and the one force ideal flow *does* predict on an accelerating body, the added (apparent) mass.

**Outline:** 6.1 when ideal flow applies ((6.1); Figs. 6.1–6.2) · 6.2 ψ and φ in 2-D, Laplace and Poisson forms with point singularities (6.2)–(6.15), boundary conditions (6.16)–(6.17), Bernoulli for pressure (6.18), polar forms (6.19)–(6.23) · 6.3 elementary flows and superposition: corner/stagnation flows (6.24)–(6.27), vortex, source, doublet (6.28)–(6.29), half-body (6.30)–(6.32), cylinder (6.33)–(6.35), d'Alembert, cylinder with circulation and lift (6.36)–(6.40), uniqueness, images (6.41), Example 6.1 (vortex near a wall, pressure signal) · 6.4 complex potential (6.42)–(6.53) · 6.5 Blasius theorem (6.54)–(6.60) and Kutta–Zhukhovsky theorem (6.61)–(6.62) · 6.6 conformal mapping (6.63)–(6.64), Zhukhovsky map, ellipse, elliptic cylinder with circulation (6.65)–(6.69) · 6.7 finite-difference Laplace solver and Gauss–Seidel iteration (6.70)–(6.73), Example 6.2 (flow through a sharp contraction) · 6.8 axisymmetric ideal flow: Stokes stream function (6.74)–(6.80), coordinate tables (6.81)–(6.88), sphere (6.89)–(6.92), source + line sink airship (6.93)–(6.95), axial singularity method · 6.9 moving sphere, surface pressure and added mass (6.96)–(6.109) · 6.10 concluding remarks · 49 exercises.

## 9. Risks, conventions, ambiguities, suspected typos

**Suspected typos / printing slips (code what is correct; record "book prints X; implemented Y"):**
1. **(6.61)** the coefficient of 1/z² inside the squared series is printed (Ud/π − Γ²/4π²); expanding (U + iΓ/2πz − d/2πz²)² gives **−(Ud/π + Γ²/4π²)** (sympy, today). The right-hand side also carries an extra outer "²" (the bracket is already the square). Neither affects the result (only the 1/z term, iUΓ/π, contributes) — teach it, do not assert the printed coefficient.
2. **(6.104)** middle expression prints "−(1/a³)u_s" inside the bracket; the gradient (6.103) evaluated at ξ = a e_ξ gives **+(1/a³)u_s**, which is what the final result (3/2)(u_s·e_ξ)e_ξ − ½u_s needs (sympy: the printed bracket gives (3/2)(u·e)e + ½u). Physical check: at the sphere's side the fluid moves at −½u_s.
3. **(6.108)** the integrand still carries "dφ" after the φ-integration (it gave the 2π that turned ρa/2 · a² into πρa³). Result −(2/3)πρa³ du_s/dt is right.
4. **§6.3 source velocities**: "direct differentiation of (6.8) produces" (u, v of the source) should read **(6.15)**.
5. **§6.6**: "rectangular hyperbolae (see Figure 6.5)" should be **Figure 6.3** (6.5 is the source).
6. **§6.4**: "Laplace equations for φ and ψ, (6.5) and (6.12), respectively" — reversed: (6.5) is for ψ, (6.12) for φ. Also "(6.43) ensures the equality of the u, v-velocity components" presumably means **(6.44)** (Cauchy–Riemann).
7. **§6.5**: "In Section 3 we demonstrated" = Section **6.3**.
8. **§6.7**: the half-point differences are called "first-order central differences"; they are **second-order accurate** (O(Δx²)); the book means "first-derivative". Our V3 test measures order 2.
9. **Example 6.2**: the FORTRAN loop that zeroes the step wall runs over I but assigns S(6, J) with J unset (should loop over **J**); harmless because S starts at zero in most compilers. **Sign and orientation conventions (highest bug risk):**
- **Γ has two signs in one chapter.** (6.6), (6.8), (6.47) and §5 of the project: Γ **counterclockwise** positive (ψ = −(Γ/2π) ln r). (6.36), (6.37)–(6.40), (6.52), (6.61)–(6.62), (6.68), Example 6.1 ("strength −Γ") use a **clockwise** Γ (the flow's circulation is −Γ) so that L = +ρUΓ; Wikipedia's Kutta–Joukowski also takes the contour clockwise. Code: `Vortex(Gamma)` always ccw; body helpers accept `Gamma_cw=` (book) or `Gamma_ccw=` (project), keyword-only; `lift_per_span(rho, U, Gamma_cw=…)` = ρUΓ_cw = −ρUΓ_ccw. Pin with a test on the stagnation points (Γ_cw > 0 moves them **down**) and on L > 0.
- **Doublet sign.** 2-D: d = Σx_i m_i points from the sink to the source; (6.29) φ = −d·x/2πr²; the cylinder needs d = −2πUa² e_x (pointing upstream, "opposing the stream"); (6.49) uses a scalar d for a dipole "−d e_x". 3-D: dipole "−d e_z" in (6.88), d = 2πa³U for the sphere; (6.92) d = −d e_z; the moving sphere (6.97) d(t) = +2πa³u_s. Code stores vectors and offers `from_book_scalar`.
- **Angles.** Polar θ from +x (downstream) in (6.33)–(6.39) and (6.89)–(6.91): the upstream stagnation point is θ = π. Fig. 6.10's axis is degrees from the *upstream* stagnation point (= π − θ); Fig. 6.8's zero-C_p angle is measured from +x at the source (our root 113.2°). (6.106)'s θ_s is measured from the sphere's *velocity* (so θ_s = π − θ of (6.91)). Use `np.arctan2`; the half-body ψ = Uy + (m/2π)θ needs θ ∈ (−π, π] with the cut downstream along the body interior (or (0, 2π) — choose one and test ψ = m/2 on both halves).
- **Stokes stream function** (m³/s) vs plane ψ (m²/s); the flux between surfaces is 2πdψ (not dψ); axisymmetric u_R = −(1/R)∂ψ/∂z (same sign as `core.SF`); spherical u_r = (1/(r² sin θ))∂ψ/∂θ.
- **Axes switch in §6.8**: the symmetry axis z is horizontal (along the stream) while earlier chapters keep z up; §6.9 uses x₃ = z and **w = z-velocity** (not the complex potential); ξ is the vector x − x_s in §6.9 but the axial coordinate along the line sink in §6.8; ζ = Zhukhovsky variable. Symbols with several meanings in this chapter: a (cylinder/sphere radius, half-body stagnation distance m/2πU, image distance, line-sink length, Zhukhovsky circle), α (corner angle π/n, conformal angle, line-sink angle), b (Zhukhovsky constant, vortex offset in exercises), B (span depth in §6.5 — **not** the Bernoulli function of ch04), d (dipole), k (line-sink density), M (added mass — not Mach), m (2-D source strength vs body mass in (6.109)), n (corner exponent vs unit normal), q (source density), Q (3-D source strength vs flow rate), R (cylindrical radius vs outer CV radius), z (complex variable vs axial coordinate).
- **Force direction.** D, L are forces *on the body*; the F of (6.54) is the force *on the fluid* (F = −B(De_x + Le_y)); in (6.98) F_s is on the sphere. Counterclockwise traversal for (6.56) (outward n).
- **Pressure** is measured relative to hydrostatic in ideal flow ((6.1)); gravity returns only in exercises (hemisphere lift-off, bubble acceleration).

**Numerical risks:**
- **Joukowski inverse branch** ((6.69)): numpy's principal `sqrt(z**2 - 4*b**2)` returns the *inside* root for Re z < 0 (checked today: \|ζ\| = 0.37 instead of 2.70 at z = −3 ± 0.5i). Use ζ = ½[z + √(z − 2b)·√(z + 2b)] (cut on the slit) and assert \|ζ\| ≥ b. Wrong-variant test required.
- **Complex log branch cuts**: vortices and sources carry multivalued φ or ψ (θ′); contour plots of ψ for a vortex are fine, but φ of a vortex jumps by Γ across the cut — place cuts outside the plotted fluid or plot velocity-derived lines; w = Az^n needs its cut outside the wedge.
- **Singular points** (element centres, re-entrant corners of Ex. 6.2, Zhukhovsky critical points ζ = ±b, slit ends): exclude from stencils; the contraction's 270° corner has infinite speed ⇒ reduced convergence order near it (report, fence).
- **Gauss–Seidel cost**: pure-Python sweeps scale as N² per sweep × O(N²) sweeps; keep the book grid tiny, use red-black vectorised sweeps or sparse direct for refined grids (notebook budget).
- **Axial singularity method** is ill-conditioned for blunt bodies and large N (a sphere is a *point* doublet — axial sources cannot reproduce it exactly); report the condition number, prefer a Rankine-oval target for V1, and fence "qualitative" if a blunt target is shown.
- **Blasius numerics**: the trapezoid on a circle converges exponentially only for analytic integrands; on a panel polygon convergence is algebraic — assert the observed rate, not a fixed tolerance.
- **Force integrals**: symmetric cancellations (drag = 0) must be checked relative to the scale ½ρU²a, not absolutely.
